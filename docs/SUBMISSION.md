## 3. Project details

**What it does.** Martian Mappers is a planning tool for walks and drives on the surface of Mars, built for astronauts and mission planners. You lay out an EVA on a map of real Mars data, the tool routes each leg around ground that is too steep and checks the plan against safety limits, and then you can walk or drive the route in 3D on measured terrain before anyone leaves Earth. Our worked example is Gale crater and lower Mount Sharp, where NASA's Curiosity rover has driven since 2012: "if a crew visited where Curiosity studied Mount Sharp" (Gale is a rover site, not a human landing candidate). The science stops are two real Curiosity drill sites in the clay-bearing Glen Torridon unit, Glen Etive 1 and Mary Anning 3, where lake mudstones may have preserved organics, plus the boxwork unit.

**How it works.** The **Plan** view is a Mars map in its native equirectangular frame, with CTX (~5 m), Viking, THEMIS and MOLA layers and HiRISE imagery. You click to add stops; each leg is routed with an A* search on the finest real elevation grid under it: HiRISE terrain models where they exist (Gale, Jezero, Gusev, Meridiani, Elysium, Ares Vallis) and MOLA everywhere else, with the resolution always shown. For every leg it reports distance, climb, steepest grade and slope class. For the whole EVA it builds a timeline with local solar time and sun elevation at each stop (NASA's Mars24 algorithm), and a radiation estimate from Curiosity's measured dose rate. It flags problems: grades above the limit, arrival after sunset, exceeding the EVA time limit, or walking further from the rover than the Apollo walk-back limit. Legs can be walked or driven in NASA's Space Exploration Vehicle at its published ~10 km/h. Plans export and import as JSON. The **Globe** view picks any site on Mars. **Rehearse 3D** walks the plan first-person on the 1 m HiRISE terrain, with the real crater rim and Mount Sharp on the horizon from MOLA; the SEV can be driven there too. A Python/GDAL pipeline turns NASA PDS products into these grids.

**Benefits and intended impact.** Crews and planners can test an EVA against real terrain, daylight and walk-back limits before committing to it, and see exactly which data each number rests on. The same workflow works at any site where HiRISE terrain models exist, and coarsely anywhere else.

**Tools.** JavaScript ES modules, three.js/WebGL and a canvas map, served by a small Node.js server; Python with GDAL, rasterio and NumPy for the data pipeline. It runs on an ordinary laptop, and with `?demo=1` fully offline from bundled data.

**Creativity.** One plan connects orbital maps, rover ground truth and a first-person rehearsal of the same route. We also checked the terrain model against the rover itself: HiRISE slope against Curiosity's measured tilt at 176 waypoints.

**Factors we considered: honesty and safety.** Every number is either measured data, a cited source or a value the planner enters, and the app says which:
- Slope limits (green ≤ 10°, amber 10–15°, red 15–20°, blocked > 20°) are NASA lunar EVA values used as stand-ins; no Mars limit is published.
- Elevation-model slope matches the rover's tilt only weakly (medians 9.0° vs 8.6°), so keep a safety margin.
- Radiation is treated as a time problem, not a map of rocks; solar particle events come on top (DONKI banner).
- Walking pace, time at stops and the EVA limit are planner inputs, because no validated Mars suited-walking speed is published.
- Outside HiRISE coverage only MOLA (~7.4 km) exists; such routes are flagged as too coarse for EVA-scale hazards.
- The HiRISE imagery colour is a tint over greyscale, and the sky colour is a rendering; both are labelled.
- Sources and fetch dates are listed in the app's "Sources & limits" panel.
- AI coding assistants helped write code and documents; the team reviewed the results, and the use is disclosed in our AI-use statement.
